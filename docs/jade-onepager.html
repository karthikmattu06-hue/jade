Jade — what an LLM serving system can give back to the grid

Power grids increasingly ask large electricity users to cut load for an hour when the system is stressed. AI datacenters would like to say yes. Jade measures, on real GPUs, what happens when you try: which part of an LLM serving stack you can turn down, how many watts that returns, how fast, and what it costs the people waiting for an answer.

Where: RIT SPORC cluster · Slurm · NVIDIA A100 Stack: vLLM · Qwen2.5 1.5B → 7B · NVML telemetry Levers: software only — no root, no firmware caps Status: week 3 · first measurements done

The question

An LLM answers a request in two phases that behave nothing alike, and modern serving systems can run them on separate machines. Prefill reads the whole prompt at once — brief, intense, compute-bound. Decode writes the answer one token at a time — long, steady, limited by memory bandwidth. If the two phases have different power signatures, then splitting them gives an operator a control surface a single server does not have. Jade asks: is that true, by how much, and what does using it cost?

Three states of one GPU, in car terms

Think of an A100 as a car with the engine running. What it burns per hour is what the grid sees; what it burns per mile is what the operator's bill sees. Both matter, and they point in opposite directions.

Parked · model loaded, no requests
≈ 40 W

Engine idling. Where a GPU lands when you stop sending it work.

Decode · writing the answer
≈ 200 W

Traffic jam: lighter load, barely moving. Cheap per hour, expensive per token.

Prefill · reading the prompt
≈ 230 W

Highway: high power, huge distance. Expensive per hour, cheap per token.

Measured on one A100 (250 W limit) serving Qwen2.5-1.5B under vLLM, September 2026. Decode costs about 27× more energy per token than prefill, yet prefill draws more power — because it processes roughly thirty times more tokens per second.

The system

What the first measurements say

Turning the engine off is the lever

  • Busy is 200–230 W; parked is ~40 W. Stop sending a GPU work and let it finish, and about 80% of its draw is gone. Nothing else comes close.
  • Batching is carpooling: 32 passengers cut fuel per passenger 25×, but the car burns slightly more per hour. An efficiency lever, not a power lever.

The speed governor only bites on the highway

  • The driver's own throttle flag says the 250 W limit is actively holding prefill back ~90% of the time, and never touches decode at small batch.
  • So a power cap is a real lever for prompt-reading and a no-op for answer-writing — which is the disaggregation argument in one line. Split the phases and you can govern one without touching the other.

The plan — four rungs

E0 · done · weeks 2–3

Three states of one GPU

Parked, decode, prefill. Energy per token per phase, decode cost versus batch size, and whether the power limit binds — measured, with the driver's own flag.

E1 · next · weeks 4–5

A half-empty road

So far the GPU was always flat out. E1 sends requests at rising rates and records power and latency at each: admit fewer requests, get how many watts back, at what cost to the ones still waiting?

E2 · weeks 6–8

Two cars

Prefill on one A100, decode on another, same traffic. Does separating the phases change what can be shed? This is also the distributed-systems core of the course deliverable.

E3 · weeks 9–11

The grid calls

Replay a real event — "cut 30% for 30 minutes" — pull the software levers, measure watts shed, ramp time, recovery, and p50/p99 latency cost. Export the lever curves. Then repeat on a 7B model for citable numbers.

Why this project

For the course

  • Distributed serving across GPUs (E2) and, if the queue allows, across nodes with Ray.
  • A real measurement instrument with its own validity checks, not a benchmark script.
  • A shedding policy evaluated on QoS cost per watt shed — the same metric family as the author's CoordDR work on moving load between datacenters.

Beyond the course

  • Emerald AI's June 2026 field paper and the BU PEAC lab's e-Energy '26 paper both list inference SLO boundaries, prefill/decode decomposition and rebound as open work. Jade measures exactly those.
  • The lever curves feed two consumers: the cloud-course operator, and an open grid simulator (FlexDC-Sim) that currently lacks LLM-serving profiles.

Deliberately out of scope: anything needing root on the node (firmware power caps, clock locks) and anything that degrades answer quality (reasoning effort, quantization). A serving operator has software levers and nothing else; that is the design, not a limitation to apologise for.
